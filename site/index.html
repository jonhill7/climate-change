How much has the climate changed, and why?

This briefing walks through the measurements, the factors that can change Earth's temperature, how scientists split the warming between human and natural causes, the main alternative explanations and how well they hold up, and what different futures look like. Chapter 1 · What has been measured

The thermometer record

Several independent groups build global temperature records from weather stations, ships and buoys. They use different methods and make different choices, yet they tell the same story: roughly flat until the early 1900s, a rise to the 1940s, a pause, and a steady climb since the 1970s.

HadCRUT5 and GISTEMP monthly values, °C vs 1850–1900

Individual months bounce around by a few tenths of a degree, largely because of El Niño and La Niña in the Pacific.

Reading the record fairly

Short wiggles are mostly natural. El Niño years (warm Pacific) are hotter and La Niña years cooler, by roughly 0.1–0.2 °C globally. Large volcanic eruptions cool the planet for one to three years.

Not all of the early warming was human-caused. The rise from about 1910 to 1945 had a sizeable natural component: a brighter Sun, few large eruptions, and ocean variability, alongside rising greenhouse gases.

2023 and 2024 jumped. 2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5 °C in most datasets. A strong El Niño explains part of the jump; the rest is still being studied (see Chapter 6). A new strong El Niño developed during 2026, which usually lifts the following year's global temperature.15

One year above 1.5 °C is not the same as breaching the Paris 1.5 °C limit, which refers to a 20-year average.

Chapter 2 · Longer context

Climate has always changed. How does today compare?

Before thermometers, temperatures are reconstructed from ice cores, tree rings, corals, lake and ocean sediments. These are less precise and usually describe averages over centuries, so they can hide brief spikes. With that caveat, here is how the present compares with notable past climates.

Global temperature in past climates

Assessed ranges relative to 1850–1900, °C (IPCC AR6)

Bars show the IPCC's assessed best estimate where one is given and whiskers the assessed range228. Past values are averages over centuries to millennia; the recent value is a 10-year average.

Bigger natural swings did happen

Ice ages came and went on cycles of about 100,000 years, paced by slow changes in Earth's orbit and amplified by CO₂ and ice. Swings of 5–7 °C between glacial and warm periods are far larger than today's change.

But usually far more slowly

Leaving the last ice age took about 10,000 years for roughly 6 °C, an average of under 0.1 °C per century. The current rate is about 0.27 °C per decade1. The IPCC found that warming since 1970 has been faster than in any other 50-year period in at least 2,000 years2.

CO₂ is outside its recent natural range

Ice cores show CO₂ between about 180 and 300 ppm over the past 800,000 years. It is now above 420 ppm, the highest in at least 2 million years2. The last time CO₂ was similar (mid-Pliocene, about 3 million years ago) the world was 2.5–4 °C warmer and seas were many metres higher.

Medieval warmth and the Little Ice Age

These were real, but large-scale reconstructions find they were not globally synchronous: different regions peaked at different centuries. The current warming is happening in about 98% of the world at once23.

Chapter 3 · What can change Earth's temperature

Human factors: greenhouse gases, pollution and land

Earth's temperature is set by a balance: sunlight absorbed versus heat radiated back to space. Anything that tips this balance is called a forcing, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). Greenhouse gases trap outgoing heat (warming); sulfate pollution reflects sunlight and brightens clouds (cooling); clearing forests changes how much light the ground reflects.

Two human effects pull in opposite directions

Greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane, nitrous oxide, CFCs and others) add about 3.6 W/m². CO₂ alone is about 2.4 W/m², and its effect is well constrained by laboratory physics and satellite measurements.

Aerosols, tiny particles mostly from burning sulfur-containing coal and oil, reflect sunlight and make clouds brighter. They offset roughly a quarter to a third of greenhouse warming. This is the largest uncertainty in the human influence: the 5–95% range runs from about −1.7 to −0.4 W/m².

As air pollution has been cleaned up (China since about 2010, and global shipping fuel rules in 2020) this cooling has weakened, which unmasks some warming.

Land use (mostly deforestation) has a small net cooling effect on reflected sunlight, but deforestation is also a major CO₂ source, counted under CO₂.

Chapter 4 · What can change Earth's temperature

Natural factors: the Sun, volcanoes, oceans and orbits

Natural factors have driven every climate change before the industrial era, so any honest account has to test them. Each one leaves a measurable trace.

Sunlight reaching Earth (total solar irradiance), W/m²

Global temperature, °C vs 1850–1900 (10-year average)

The Sun brightens and dims by about 0.1% over each 11-year cycle. It brightened slightly in the first half of the 1900s and has been flat to slightly dimmer since the 1980s, while temperature rose most steeply. Satellites have measured solar output directly since 1978. Solar data: IGCC composite of satellite and reconstructed irradiance1.

Stratospheric aerosol optical depth: how much sunlight volcanic haze blocks high in the atmosphere. Large eruptions cool the globe by 0.1–0.5 °C for one to three years, then the haze settles out. They cannot cause a long-term warming trend; a quiet volcanic period can only cause a small, temporary rebound1.

Heat gained by the oceans since 1971, zettajoules

El Niño / La Niña (ENSO) swaps heat between the deep Pacific and the surface every 2–7 years, moving global temperature up or down by about 0.1–0.2 °C.

The Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) and Atlantic Multidecadal Variability (AMO/AMV) are slower patterns lasting decades. They likely contributed to the 1940s warmth and the slower warming of 1998–2012.

The key test: if the surface were warming because the ocean was releasing stored heat, ocean heat content would fall. Instead the oceans have gained a very large amount of heat, about 500 zettajoules since 19711, roughly the energy of 15 times the world's yearly energy use every year. The whole system is gaining energy, which points to an outside push rather than heat being shuffled around.

Milankovitch cycles

Slow wobbles in Earth's tilt (41,000-year cycle), the shape of its orbit (100,000 years) and the direction its axis points (about 23,000 years) shift where and when sunlight falls. They pace the ice ages.

The current orbital trend is slight cooling

Northern summer sunshine has been slowly declining for about 10,000 years, which is why temperatures drifted gently downward through the last few thousand years before the industrial era.

Far too slow to matter this century

Orbital changes act over thousands of years. Their effect over 150 years is negligible. Studies suggest the next ice age would not begin for at least 50,000 years, and later if CO₂ stays high25.

Net natural forcing today: solar plus volcanic adds about +0.3 W/m² relative to 1750, versus about +3.1 W/m² from human activity1. The natural number is mostly a quiet volcanic period compared with the long-term average, which is temporary.
Chapter 5 · Putting it together

How much of the warming is human-caused?

Attribution studies take the measured forcings, physical models of how the climate responds, and the observed pattern of warming across space and time, then ask which combination of causes best explains what was observed. Three independent methods are updated every year.

Fingerprints that separate the causes

Upper atmosphere is cooling

Greenhouse gases trap heat low down, so the stratosphere cools while the surface warms. A brighter Sun would warm both. Satellites show strong stratospheric cooling31.

Nights and winters warm faster

Extra greenhouse gases reduce heat loss at night and in winter more than they add heat by day, the pattern seen in station records.

Less heat escapes at CO₂'s wavelengths

Satellite and ground spectrometers have measured the extra infrared trapped specifically in the CO₂ absorption bands17.

The added carbon is fossil carbon

Fossil carbon is low in carbon-13 and has no carbon-14. Both ratios in the air are falling as CO₂ rises, and atmospheric oxygen is declining in step with combustion32.

Chapter 6 · Testing other explanations

Alternative explanations, weighed on the evidence

Some of these ideas come from published scientists, others from public debate. Each card states the claim in its strongest form, the evidence that supports it, the evidence against it, and the current best quantitative estimate. The last two cards cover claims that the situation is worse than the mainstream estimate, because skepticism should run in both directions.

“It's a natural trend, a recovery from the Little Ice Age”

Could the warming since 1850 be mainly a natural rebound or long cycle?~0.0 °Cnatural forcing contribution, ±0.2 °C internal variability

The claim: climate always changes. The 1850s were at the tail of the Little Ice Age (roughly 1450–1850), and the warming since is a return toward medieval or Roman-era warmth, possibly part of a millennial cycle.

Evidence for

  • The Little Ice Age was real, especially in the Northern Hemisphere, and was linked to clusters of volcanic eruptions and low solar activity (for example the Maunder Minimum, 1645–1715).
  • Part of the early 20th-century warming (about 1910–1945) had natural causes: a brighter Sun, few eruptions and ocean variability.
  • Paleoclimate records show earlier warm periods in some regions.

Evidence against

  • "Recovery" is not a physical mechanism. Warming requires extra energy; the measured natural drivers (Sun, volcanoes, orbit) show no sustained upward push since 1950.
  • The ocean is gaining heat at every depth measured, so heat is not just being moved around within the system.
  • Orbital forcing has been nudging the climate cooler for several thousand years.
  • Past warm periods were regional and occurred at different times in different places; the present warming is global and simultaneous23.
  • Recent decades are warmer than any multi-century period in more than 100,000 years2.
Current best estimateNatural forcings (solar and volcanic) contributed +0.04 °C (5–95%: −0.1 to +0.2 °C) of the warming to 2025; internal variability likely adds or subtracts up to about 0.2 °C12. Natural factors matter a lot for year-to-year and decade-to-decade swings, much less for the century-scale trend.

“The Sun is the main driver”

Including the idea that cosmic rays, modulated by the Sun, change cloud cover±0.1 °Csolar and volcanic contribution, 1850–1900 to 2010–2019

The claim: solar activity rose through the 20th century to a "grand maximum", and either directly (brighter Sun) or indirectly (fewer cosmic rays, fewer low clouds) caused most of the warming. Some researchers argue that the solar records chosen by the IPCC understate solar variability, and that with other solar reconstructions and rural-only temperature records, the Sun could explain much of the warming21.

Evidence for

  • The Sun did brighten slightly from the late 1800s to the mid-1900s, overlapping with early warming.
  • Solar lows coincide with some cold periods in the paleo record.
  • Reconstructions of solar output before satellites disagree, and some (for example those built on the ACRIM satellite composite) show larger changes.
  • Cosmic rays can seed tiny particles in the laboratory (the CERN CLOUD experiment confirmed the mechanism exists)16.

Evidence against

  • Satellites have measured solar output directly since 1978. It shows no upward trend (if anything a slight decline) while global temperature rose about 0.9 °C.
  • A solar cause would warm the whole atmosphere. The stratosphere has cooled, the signature of greenhouse gases31.
  • CLOUD found the cosmic-ray effect on cloud-forming particles is too small to matter for climate16; cloud cover has not tracked the cosmic-ray record.
  • The whole solar cycle changes forcing by only about 0.1–0.2 W/m², versus about 3 W/m² of human forcing.
Current best estimateSolar forcing today is about +0.1 W/m² relative to 1750, against about +3.1 W/m² from human activity1. IPCC AR6 assessed the combined solar and volcanic contribution to warming from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 at −0.1 to +0.1 °C2. A minority of researchers dispute the choice of solar record; this remains an active, if small, debate.

“Ocean cycles like the AMO and PDO explain it”

Could slow ocean oscillations be lifting surface temperatures?±0.2 °Clikely range for internal variability since 1850–1900

The claim: the Atlantic and Pacific have multidecadal rhythms (roughly 60–70 years) that happened to be in warm phases recently. Some authors fit cycles of this kind to the temperature record and conclude that the human contribution is overstated.

Evidence for

  • ENSO clearly drives year-to-year swings; about a third of the 2022-to-2023 jump has been attributed to the shift from La Niña to El Niño12.
  • The 1998–2012 "slowdown" in surface warming was linked to a cool-phase Pacific and more heat going into the deep ocean.
  • North Atlantic variability likely contributed to the mid-century warm period and to recent Arctic warming.

Evidence against

  • Oscillations move heat around; they do not create it. If the oceans were warming the air, ocean heat content would fall. It has risen by about 500 zettajoules since 1971.
  • All ocean basins and depths are warming together, which does not fit a regional see-saw.
  • Instrumental records cover only two or three cycles of a 60-year rhythm, so statistical cycle fits are easy to overfit and have not predicted the post-2010 warming.
  • Earth's measured energy imbalance (more energy in than out) roughly doubled from the mid-2000s to the early 2020s29.
Current best estimateIPCC AR6 assessed the likely contribution of internal variability to warming since 1850–1900 at −0.2 to +0.2 °C2. Ocean cycles are important for explaining wiggles and decade-scale pauses and surges, not the long-term rise.

“Water vapour from the 2022 Hunga Tonga eruption caused the recent heat”

Did the underwater volcano's huge injection of water warm the planet in 2023–2024?≈ 0 °Cnet global effect; slight cooling in 2022–23 is the more common finding

The claim: in January 2022 the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha'apai volcano blasted an unprecedented amount of water into the stratosphere, about 146 million tonnes, raising stratospheric water vapour by roughly 10%7. Water vapour is a greenhouse gas, and the record heat of 2023–2024 followed.

Evidence for

  • The water injection was genuinely unprecedented in the satellite era, and it lingers for years.
  • The water vapour itself does warm: IGCC estimates its forcing at about +0.15 W/m² in 2023–20241.
  • An early modelling study suggested the water could raise the chance of temporarily exceeding 1.5 °C in the following years11.

Evidence against

  • The eruption also released sulfur dioxide, which formed reflective sulfate haze. Studies find this cooling equalled or outweighed the water's warming, especially in 2022–2023.
  • A 2026 multi-model study found a net global forcing of −0.19 ± 0.06 W/m² for 2022–2023 (cooling), with sulfate dominating9.
  • Satellite-based estimates found the eruption cooled the Southern Hemisphere by about 0.1 °C in 2022–20238; another estimate put the cooling at under 0.04 °C10.
  • Even taken alone, the water vapour forcing is about 5% of the human forcing, and the long-term warming started decades before 2022.

“CO₂'s effect is saturated, or climate sensitivity is low”

Does adding more CO₂ still matter, and how much warming per doubling?3 °Cper CO₂ doubling, likely 2.5–4 °C (IPCC AR6)

The claim: CO₂ already absorbs nearly all the infrared it can, so more CO₂ adds little. A related, more technical claim is that the climate's sensitivity (warming per doubling of CO₂) is at the low end, around 1.5–2 °C, based on observed warming and energy budgets18.

Evidence for

  • The centre of CO₂'s main absorption band is saturated, and CO₂'s effect grows logarithmically: each doubling adds about the same forcing (about 3.9 W/m²), so each extra ppm matters a little less than the last.
  • Some energy-budget studies using observed warming found best estimates near 1.5–2.2 °C18.
  • A subset of the latest climate models do run "too hot" compared with observations, a problem the modelling community itself flagged20; IPCC AR6 did not rely on raw model averages for this reason.

Evidence against

  • Saturation at the band centre does not stop the effect: added CO₂ absorbs more in the band's wings and raises the altitude from which heat escapes, where it is colder. The growth in trapped infrared has been measured directly at the surface17.
  • Simple energy-budget estimates are biased low because the recent warming pattern (a cool eastern Pacific) is unusual and expected to change.
  • Independent lines of evidence (physics of feedbacks, the historical record, and past climates like the last ice age) converge near 3 °C19.
Current best estimateEquilibrium climate sensitivity: best estimate 3 °C, likely 2.5–4 °C, very likely 2–5 °C. The shorter-term transient response is about 1.8 °C (likely 1.4–2.2 °C)2. Even at the low end, warming continues while emissions continue; a lower sensitivity buys time but does not stop it.

“The temperature record is distorted by cities and adjustments”

Are urban heat islands or data adjustments creating the trend?Smallurban effect on the global trend; adjustments reduce the global trend

The claim: many weather stations sit in growing towns or near pavement and air conditioners, and raw data are "adjusted" by agencies, so the warming may be an artefact.

Evidence for

  • Urban heat islands are real: cities can be several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside, especially at night.
  • Station siting is sometimes poor, and stations move or change equipment, so adjustments are necessary and involve judgement.
  • Land records in some countries were adjusted upward over time, which understandably attracts scrutiny.

Evidence against

  • About 70% of the surface is ocean, with no cities, and ocean records show warming.
  • Satellite measurements of the lower atmosphere, including the series run by researchers often skeptical of mainstream estimates (UAH), also show warming.
  • Rural-only station subsets show similar warming to the full network.
  • Taken together, adjustments reduce the long-term global warming trend, mainly because early ship measurements are corrected upward30.
  • Berkeley Earth was set up in part to test these concerns, with some funding from foundations associated with skeptics, and confirmed the trend5.
  • Independent indicators agree: rising seas, shrinking glaciers and Arctic sea ice, earlier springs, rising ocean heat.
Current best estimateUrbanization contributes a small fraction of the measured global land trend and much less of the land-plus-ocean global average2. The four records in Chapter 1 agree closely despite different methods.

“The CO₂ rise is natural, from oceans or volcanoes”

Is human activity really the source of the extra CO₂?~100%of the rise since 1750 attributed to humans

The claim: nature emits about 20 times more CO₂ each year than people do (from oceans, plants and soils), and in past ice-age cycles CO₂ followed temperature, so the current rise may be a consequence of warming rather than its cause. Volcanoes are also sometimes cited.

Evidence for

  • Natural flows of carbon are much larger than human emissions in both directions.
  • In ice-age cycles, CO₂ changes sometimes lagged temperature by centuries: orbital changes warmed the oceans, which released CO₂, which then amplified the warming.
  • Warmer water holds less CO₂, so warming oceans do release some.

Evidence against

  • Mass balance: people emit about 43 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year, and the atmosphere gains only about half of that. Nature as a whole is a net absorber.
  • The ocean is absorbing CO₂ and becoming more acidic, the opposite of what would happen if it were the source.
  • Isotopes fingerprint the source: falling carbon-13 and carbon-14 ratios match fossil carbon, and oxygen is declining in step with burning32.
  • Volcanoes emit roughly 0.3–0.4 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year, around 1% of human emissions24.
Current best estimateThe rise from about 278 ppm in 1750 to over 420 ppm today is attributed essentially entirely to human emissions. The ice-age lag is real, and it shows that CO₂ acts as both a cause and an amplifier of warming2.

“Warming is accelerating much faster than the mainstream estimate”

A claim from the other direction: are official estimates too reassuring?~0.27 °Cper decade human-induced rate (IGCC); faster than before, debated how much

The claim: some scientists, notably James Hansen and colleagues, argue that climate sensitivity is about 4.5 °C, that cleaner air is unmasking more warming than assumed, and that warming has accelerated to around 0.3–0.4 °C per decade22. The 2023–2024 jump is presented as evidence.

Evidence for

  • The human-induced warming rate has increased, to about 0.27 °C per decade over the last ten years1, up from about 0.18 °C per decade in the 1980s–2000s.
  • Earth's energy imbalance roughly doubled between the mid-2000s and early 2020s29.
  • 2023 had unusually low reflectivity; low clouds decreased and the planet absorbed about 1 W/m² more energy than the prior 20-year average13.

Evidence against

  • Much of the 2023–2024 jump is explained by El Niño, Indian Ocean and Atlantic variability, the solar maximum and shipping fuel rules, with modest unexplained residual1227.
  • An acceleration of this size is within what mainstream models projected once falling aerosol pollution is included.
  • Whether the cloud decrease is a lasting feedback or temporary variability is not yet known.
Current best estimateWarming has sped up, consistent with rising greenhouse forcing plus declining aerosol cooling. Whether sensitivity is in the upper part of the IPCC range is an open question that the next decade of data should help settle.

“We are on track for the worst-case scenario”

Another claim from the other direction: is 4–5 °C by 2100 the expected outcome?~2.8 °Cby 2100 with current policies (UNEP 2025)

The claim: the high-emissions scenario (SSP5-8.5, or the older RCP8.5) is "business as usual", so 4–5 °C of warming this century is where we are headed.

Evidence for

  • Global emissions have not yet started a sustained decline.
  • Carbon-cycle feedbacks (thawing permafrost, weakening land and ocean sinks) could add warming beyond central estimates.
  • High-sensitivity outcomes cannot be ruled out, and they would push moderate emissions toward higher warming.

Evidence against

  • SSP5-8.5 assumes a large expansion of coal use that energy trends no longer support; analysts consider it unlikely as a baseline26.
  • Cheap solar, wind and batteries have bent energy projections downward over the past decade.
  • Current-policy projections are near 2.8 °C by 2100, and 2.3–2.5 °C if national pledges are fully delivered14.
Current best estimateThe most likely path on current policies lies between the middle scenarios SSP2-4.5 and SSP3-7.0 (about 2.5–3 °C by 2100), with real uncertainty from future policy, technology and the climate system's response.
Chapter 7 · Other measures of change

Rising seas and a warming ocean

Surface temperature is one gauge. More than 90% of the extra heat goes into the ocean, and sea level integrates both ocean warming (water expands) and melting land ice. These are slow, steady indicators with little year-to-year noise.

Arctic sea ice

September Arctic sea-ice extent has declined about 12–13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979. Summer ice is thinner and younger.

Glaciers and ice sheets

Glaciers worldwide and the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have been losing mass, now contributing more to sea-level rise than ocean expansion.

Extremes

Hot extremes have become more frequent and intense over most land regions; the IPCC finds human influence is the main driver of this. Evidence for changes in droughts and storms is more mixed and varies by region2.

Chapter 8 · What may come

Future warming depends mostly on future emissions

Projections are not predictions of what people will choose. They are "if-then" statements: if emissions follow a given path, then warming is likely to fall in a given range. The range within each path reflects uncertainty in the climate's response.

Warming stops rising when CO₂ emissions reach net zero

Temperature is closely tied to the total CO₂ emitted. Once emissions reach net zero, warming is expected to roughly level off, though sea level keeps rising for centuries.

Low-probability, high-impact outcomes

Ice-sheet instability could push sea-level rise toward 2 m by 2100 in a high-emissions world; this cannot be ruled out but is not the central estimate. A slowdown of the Atlantic overturning circulation is likely this century; an abrupt collapse is considered unlikely before 2100 but is debated2.

The next few years

With a strong El Niño under way in late 202615, 2027 has a high chance of setting a new annual record. A single record year says more about El Niño than about the long-term rate.

Chapter 9 · Summary

What we know, what's likely, and what's uncertain

●●● Very well established

  • The world has warmed about 1.3–1.4 °C since 1850–1900; independent records agree.
  • The rise in CO₂ is from human activity.
  • Greenhouse gases trap heat; this is laboratory physics, measured from space and the ground.
  • Most of the warming since 1950 is human-caused. Best estimates put the human share at roughly all of it, with natural factors close to zero net.
  • The oceans are gaining heat and sea level is rising faster.

●●○ Likely, with a range

  • Doubling CO₂ brings about 2.5–4 °C of eventual warming.
  • Current policies point to roughly 2.5–3 °C by 2100.
  • Aerosol pollution has hidden roughly 0.3–0.5 °C of warming; cleaner air reveals some of it.
  • Hot extremes will keep becoming more frequent with each increment of warming.

●○○ Genuinely uncertain

  • Exactly how strong aerosol cooling and cloud feedbacks are.
  • How much of the 2023–2024 jump reflects a lasting change.
  • Timing and likelihood of tipping points (ice sheets, Atlantic circulation, permafrost).
  • Regional details, such as rainfall changes in many places.
  • Future emissions, which depend on policy, technology and economics rather than physics.
What would change this picture? A sustained decline in ocean heat content while greenhouse gases keep rising; a stratosphere that stopped cooling; measured solar output rising enough to match the warming; or warming that stalled for several decades despite rising forcing. None of these has been observed, but they are the observations a scientist would look for.
